## Lecture

### 1. What the sensors actually measure

**2D LiDAR** — range to the nearest surface, along a set of rays, in a single horizontal plane. That
is all. MRider's profile, as modelled in the URDF:

| | |
|---|---|
| Samples | 720 (0.5° resolution) |
| Field of view | 360° |
| Range | 12 m |
| Rate | 10 Hz |

**Camera** — colour and texture, projected onto a plane. No depth.

| | |
|---|---|
| Resolution | 640 × 480 |
| Horizontal FOV | 1.396 rad (~80°) |
| Rate | 30 Hz |

!!! danger "What a horizontal-plane scanner cannot see"

    A 2D LiDAR sees **one slice** of the world at its mounting height. It is blind to:

    - anything below the plane — a kerb, a step, a hole
    - anything above it — a table edge, a low shelf, an open drawer
    - anything transparent or mirror-like — glass doors read as open space

    Lab 4 had you find the blind spots by construction. Now the consequence: SLAM and Nav2 both
    treat "no return" as "free space." **The map does not distinguish *nothing there* from *nothing
    visible*.**

    And on this vehicle there is a further gap you already know: **the LiDAR faces forward and there
    is no rear sensing at all.**

### 2. Rolling shutter

A rolling-shutter sensor exposes the image **row by row** rather than all at once. If the camera
moves during that scan, the image shears — vertical lines lean, and the lean depends on speed.

[The camera ADR](../../design/sensors.md) chooses a **$30 rolling-shutter USB camera for semester 1**,
with global shutter deferred to phase 2.

!!! success "Why that is a good decision rather than a cheap one"

    At ≤ walking pace, on a vehicle whose limiting factor is feedback quality rather than image
    quality, shear is small and the money is better spent elsewhere. The ADR says so explicitly, and
    names the condition under which it flips.

    **Phase 2 is behavior cloning**, where the camera stops being a sensor you look at and becomes
    the *input to a learned policy*. A speed-dependent geometric distortion in the training data is
    then a systematic bias the network will happily learn — and you will not see it in the loss
    curve.

    The decision is not "rolling shutter is fine." It is *"rolling shutter is fine for what we are
    doing this semester, and here is what would change our minds."*

### 3. Intrinsics — the camera's own geometry

Intrinsic calibration recovers focal length, principal point, and lens distortion — the mapping from
3D rays to pixels. Without it you cannot turn a pixel into a direction, so you cannot relate the
image to anything else in the world.

[calibration.md §3](../../design/calibration.md) has the procedure: a checkerboard, many views at
varied distance and angle, and a stored `camera_info`.

**In simulation this is free**, because the simulated camera is a perfect pinhole and its
`camera_info` is exact. That is worth noticing rather than enjoying — **the twin cannot teach you
that intrinsic calibration is hard**, because in the twin it does not exist as a problem.

### 4. Extrinsics — where the sensor is

Intrinsics are about the sensor. **Extrinsics are about the vehicle**: the rigid transform from each
sensor frame to `base_link`.

In simulation you typed those numbers into the URDF and they became true. On hardware it runs the
other way — the sensor is bolted somewhere and you must *measure* where.

The staged procedure:

| Stage | |
|---|---|
| **4.2 Coarse** | Measure with a ruler from the `base_link` origin. Good to ~1 cm |
| **4.3 LiDAR refinement** | Drive parallel to a known flat wall; the scan must be parallel too. Corrects yaw and lateral offset |
| **4.4 Camera→LiDAR** | Cross-sensor: project scan points into the image and check they land on the objects they came from |

!!! quote "Lab 4 already showed you what a 5 cm error costs"

    Nothing errors. The LiDAR publishes, TF is complete, SLAM runs — and walls observed from
    different headings no longer land on top of each other.

    A **constant** mounting offset produces a **heading-dependent** map error, because that offset
    points in a different world direction as the vehicle rotates. Which is why the acceptance gate is
    stated as *repeated observations of the same wall agreeing within 10 cm*, rather than as
    something about the sensor.

### 5. Frames, and the one that is easy to get wrong

Every measurement arrives in a frame, and the timestamp matters as much as the transform.

[calibration.md §6](../../design/calibration.md) covers time synchronisation, and it is not a
formality: if the LiDAR's timestamps are offset from the vehicle's, TF will happily interpolate a
pose for the wrong instant, and every scan gets placed slightly wrong — **in a way that scales with
speed**, so it looks like a mechanical problem rather than a clock problem.

This is also why [ADR-SW1](../../design/software.md) insists on one transport and one clock for the
DBW link.
