## Mid-semester presentation

**12 minutes + 5 minutes of questions.** Everyone on the team speaks. Present to your classmates,
not to me — the other three tracks are the audience that matters, because they are the ones who have
to merge with you.

### Rubric (100 points)

| | Points | |
|---|---:|---|
| **Subsystem demonstrated** | 30 | Something works, live or recorded. Partial is expected at Week 8 |
| **Evidence quality** | 25 | Numbers, plots, logs. Measured, not asserted |
| **Interface clarity** | 20 | What you need from other tracks and what you will give them — concretely, with dates |
| **Risk honesty** | 15 | What is going wrong, and what you will do about it |
| **Delivery** | 10 | Clear, on time, everyone speaks |

### Suggested structure

1. **What you own** (1 min) — the deliverable, in one sentence
2. **What works** (4 min) — demo it. A 30-second screen recording beats a slide describing it
3. **The evidence** (3 min) — your numbers, and how you obtained them
4. **Your interfaces** (2 min) — what you need, from whom, by when; what you will hand over
5. **What is going wrong** (2 min) — honestly

!!! warning "The two most common ways to lose points"

    **Presenting a plan instead of a result.** You are three weeks into track work. "We researched
    the options" is a Week 6 answer. Show what you built, even if it is small and half-broken.

    **Not having talked to the track you depend on.** Interface clarity is 20 points precisely
    because it is the thing that fails silently until Week 13. If your slide says "we need the
    measured wheelbase" and Chassis has never heard that, both teams lose points.

!!! success "Risk honesty is 15 points, and it is free"

    A team that says *"our angle sensor reads garbage past 300° and we do not yet know why"* scores
    above one that shows a clean demo and cannot explain how it works.

    This is the course standard, applied at the halfway mark: **honest failure outscores a lucky
    success.** Nobody expects a working subsystem at Week 8.

## Lecture — safety

### 1. Authority is a ladder, and it is electrical

You met the four layers in Week 3. Here they are with their failure modes:

| Layer | Mechanism | Works if the firmware has crashed? |
|---|---|---|
| **E-stop** | Hardwired contactor cuts traction power | **Yes** |
| **Relay MUX** | DPDT coil drops → reverts to stock mechanical steering | **Yes** |
| **RC signal MUX** | Hardware multiplexer on the servo pulse | **Yes** |
| **SBUS override** | RC frames into the Teensy, closed-loop | No |

**Default is STOCK on every power-up and every fault.** The MUX holds the DBW position only while
energised, so losing power *is* reverting to manual. Safety is the resting state, not a state you
have to reach.

### 2. The failsafe matrix

Nine rows in [safety.md §2](../../design/safety.md). Every one is testable on the bench, and the
Electrical track will test every one at Stage 3.

The pattern across all of them: **detect within a bounded time, resolve to a defined safe state,
require an explicit re-arm.**

| Loss | Detection | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Command loss | setpoint stale > 500 ms | ESTOP, throttle → 0, steering centred then de-energised |
| USB link loss | no micro-ROS session | Teensy enters ESTOP autonomously |
| RC loss | SBUS frame timeout | ESTOP |
| Brownout | logic-rail undervoltage | MUX coil drops → **STOCK** |
| E-stop pressed | hardwired | traction cut, MUX → STOCK |
| Sabertooth command loss | serial timeout | motors stop |
| Steering at limit | stall detected | clamp effort toward centre only |
| Firmware hang | Sabertooth timeout + watchdog | motors stop, Teensy reset to neutral |
| Angle sensor fault | plausibility check each loop | ESTOP — **never run the loop on a bad angle** |

!!! danger "Row 2 is a genuine regression, and the design record says so"

    Under the superseded Pixhawk design, USB carried **feedback only** — the steering setpoint
    arrived separately as servo PWM, so a USB dropout left steering still tracking.

    **Under D3 the same link carries the setpoint,** so a USB dropout removes it. That is worse in
    one specific respect, and it was accepted knowingly, on the grounds that the failure is now
    detected in one place with one timeout and resolves to a defined safe state — rather than to
    *"keep tracking a setpoint whose author is gone"*.

    **A stale-setpoint-with-live-actuator state is more dangerous than a stop.**

    This is what a design record is for. The regression is written down, argued, and given a
    measurement: log USB session stability over ≥ 30 minutes, and *if dropouts occur at all, treat
    it as a blocking defect, not a nuisance.*

### 3. The state machine

Three modes, in `DbwStatus`:

```
        ┌─────────┐  fault / stale / RC loss   ┌──────────────┐
        │  ESTOP  │◀───────────────────────────│  AUTONOMOUS  │
        └────┬────┘                            └──────▲───────┘
             │ explicit re-arm                        │ operator hands over
             ▼                                        │
        ┌──────────────┐──────────────────────────────┘
        │  MANUAL_RC   │
        └──────────────┘
```

Two properties worth stating explicitly:

- **ESTOP is entered automatically, and left only explicitly.** No fault clears itself into motion.
- **Any set fault bit is a reason to refuse to leave ESTOP.** Which is why the bitfield names six
  causes rather than being a boolean — the supervisor needs to know *which*.

### 4. FMEA — the one that is severity 5

Of everything in the FMEA, one failure mode scores the maximum:

> **Magnet wrap** — the sensed shaft rotates past one turn. The angle folds back, and the loop
> drives the wrong way, ***silently***.

Severity 5, and note *why*: not because the consequence is worse than the others, but because it is
**silent**. The sensor reads a perfectly plausible value. Nothing detects it. The loop does exactly
what it was told, in the wrong direction.

The mitigation is mechanical, not software:

> **Load-side mounting (±22.5°) makes wrap mechanically impossible.**

!!! quote "This is the strongest engineering idea in the whole design"

    A software check for wrap would have to distinguish a wrapped reading from a legitimate one, and
    for a rotary sensor those can be identical. So the design **eliminates the failure mode instead
    of detecting it** — mount the sensor where the shaft physically cannot rotate far enough to wrap.

    You have now seen the same move twice. Week 6: the Lisbon declination was not fixed, the
    dependency on it was deleted. Here: wrap is not detected, it is made impossible.

    **When you cannot reliably detect a failure, look for a way to make it unreachable.**

### 5. The staged bring-up protocol

> **Bench before vehicle; wheels-off before wheels-on; walking pace before anything faster.**
> No stage begins until the previous stage passes.

| Stage | What | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Bench, no motors | `/mitt/dbw/status` ≥ 50 Hz; **zero USB dropouts over 30 min** |
| **1** | Bench, steering motor only | Closed loop ≥ 200 Hz, no runaway, freewheel on power cut. **E4 decision point** |
| **2** | Bench, both channels + RC | **Both override layers demonstrated — including the MUX with the Teensy deliberately halted** |
| **3** | Relay MUX + E-stop, wheels off | Every failsafe row, incl. E-stop **with the laptop powered off** |
| **4** | Vehicle, wheels on stands | Full integration; no logic-rail brownout under steering stall |
| **5** | Ground, walking pace, operator alongside | E-stop on the move. Only then does autonomy engage |

!!! danger "This is a hard gate in this course, not advice"

    A team that skips a stage does not lose marks. **It does not demo.**

    The protocol is staged specifically so a class can work on a vehicle heavy enough to hurt
    someone without an instructor watching every individual action. That only works if nobody
    treats it as optional.

    Note Stage 2 in particular: the hardware MUX must be demonstrated **with the Teensy deliberately
    halted.** That is the D3 condition, and the record is explicit — it *must be demonstrated, not
    assumed.*

## Cross-team dependency review

Every ask, from every charter, on one board:

- Who needs what, from whom, by when
- Is the date still real?
- **What is nobody claiming?** Gaps between charters are how Week 13 fails

If your track is going to miss a merge date, this is the session to say so. Announcing a slip at
Week 8 is professional. Discovering it at Merge 3 is not.

## Peer evaluation 1

In class, confidential. For each teammate including yourself, 1–5 on contribution, reliability, and
collaboration — plus free text on **what this person did that the git history would not show me.**
Documentation, debugging someone else's problem, and asking the question that saved a week are all
real work that leaves no commit.

Peer evaluation can adjust an individual's team-component grade by up to ±15%.

!!! note "If your team has a problem, now is when it is fixable"

    At Week 8 there are seven weeks left. At Week 15 there is nothing I can do but adjust grades,
    which helps nobody build anything.
