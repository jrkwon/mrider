## Lecture

### 1. Feedback control, and PID

You have a setpoint and a measurement. The error is the difference. A controller turns error into
effort:

$$ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau + K_d \frac{de}{dt} $$

| Term | Responds to | Buys you | Costs you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P** | error now | Speed of response | Steady-state offset; oscillation if too high |
| **I** | accumulated error | Kills steady-state offset | Windup, overshoot, sluggishness |
| **D** | rate of change | Damping | Amplifies sensor noise |

The MRider steering loop is a **position** loop: setpoint is a road-wheel angle in radians, the
measurement comes from the load-side absolute sensor, and the effort is a signed PWM command to the
gearmotor.

!!! danger "D on a noisy encoder is how you cook a gearmotor"

    Differentiating a noisy signal amplifies the noise. On a mechanical steering column that shows up
    as audible chatter and real current draw — you are not just seeing noise, you are *actuating* it.

    Filter the derivative, or start at PD with a small D, or use derivative-on-measurement rather
    than derivative-on-error so a setpoint step does not produce a spike.

### 2. Where the loop lives, and why

[ADR E](../../design/dbw.md) puts the steering loop **on the Teensy**, not in ROS. The reasoning is
the timing argument from Week 7: Linux cannot promise 200 Hz, and a position loop that *usually*
makes its deadline is a position loop that oscillates unpredictably under load.

There is a second reason worth stating: **the loop must survive the laptop.** If the USB link drops,
a loop running in ROS is simply gone. A loop on the Teensy is still there — and can centre the wheels
and de-energise, which is exactly what failsafe row 1 requires.

!!! info "E4 is still live, and Stage 1 is where it fires"

    > If the loop cannot hold **≤ 1° steady-state with no sustained oscillation** at bring-up Stage 1,
    > adopt the motion-controller daughterboard **rather than continuing to tune.**

    Electrical track: that is your number. Note it is a *pre-registered* trigger — written before the
    attempt, so the decision to stop tuning is not made by someone who is three days into tuning and
    emotionally invested in it.

### 3. Path tracking is a different problem

The outer loop does not have a setpoint in the same sense. It has a *path*, and the vehicle is
somewhere near it.

**Pure pursuit** is the classic geometric answer. Pick a point on the path a lookahead distance *L_d*
ahead, and compute the arc from the vehicle's current pose to that point:

$$ \kappa = \frac{2\sin\alpha}{L_d} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta = \arctan(\kappa L) $$

where α is the angle to the lookahead point and κ is curvature. Then steer to δ.

It has no error term, no gains to tune, and no integrator. It is pure geometry — which is why it maps
so naturally onto a car, and why it cannot ask for a manoeuvre the car cannot perform.

**Lookahead distance is the one real knob:**

| L_d | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Too short | Tracks tightly, oscillates, chases every wiggle in the path |
| Too long | Smooth and stable, but **cuts corners** and may clip obstacles |

### 4. What "Regulated" adds

`RegulatedPurePursuitController` extends the above in the ways that matter on a real vehicle:

- **Curvature regulation** — slow down in tight turns, automatically
- **Proximity regulation** — slow down near obstacles
- **Velocity-scaled lookahead** — longer lookahead when moving fast, shorter when slow

Read MRider's configuration, and note that every value has a reason:

```yaml
FollowPath:
  plugin: "nav2_regulated_pure_pursuit_controller::RegulatedPurePursuitController"
  desired_linear_vel: 0.6       # well under the 1.4 m/s cap; <= walking speed
  lookahead_dist: 0.8
  min_lookahead_dist: 0.4
  max_lookahead_dist: 1.2
  use_velocity_scaled_lookahead_dist: true
  lookahead_time: 1.5
  min_turning_radius: 1.6
  use_rotate_to_heading: false
  allow_reversing: true
```

!!! warning "`use_rotate_to_heading` and `allow_reversing` are mutually exclusive"

    Set both `true` and Nav2 **refuses to start**, with an error naming both parameters. That is
    Nav2 protecting you: rotate-to-heading means "stop and spin to face the right way," which this
    vehicle cannot do at all.

    So `use_rotate_to_heading: false` is not a preference. It is the only legal value for a car —
    and it is what makes `allow_reversing` available.

### 5. The constraint, one more time

`min_turning_radius: 1.6` is R_min = 1.52 m plus margin. Its consequence reaches all the way to the
goal checker:

```yaml
goal_checker:
  xy_goal_tolerance: 0.6
  yaw_goal_tolerance: 0.6
```

Those look sloppy. They are geometry.

!!! quote "Finding F-N3 — the goal checker was asking for a parallel park"

    At 0.25 m / 0.35 rad the vehicle arrived within 0.75 m and then **orbited the goal
    indefinitely** — (-4.19,-1.46), (-5.41,0.43), (-2.28,0.81), (-4.51,-1.35), repeating.

    **It never converged and it never failed.** No error, no timeout, no diagnostic. Just a car
    driving in circles around a point it could not reach.

    A vehicle with a 1.52 m turning radius and no in-place rotation cannot reduce a 0.75 m pose
    error. It can only loop past at ≥ 1.52 m radius and try again. 0.6 m is ~40% of the turning
    radius and is achievable in one pass.

    The record calls this **"a workaround, not the fix"** — and it was right. The real fix was the
    planner, which is Week 12.

Note the shape of that failure. It is the same family you met in Weeks 1, 3, and 4: **the system did
not report a problem, because from its own point of view there was not one.** Every component did
exactly what it was configured to do.
