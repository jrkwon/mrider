## Lecture

### 1. SLAM is circular, and that is the difficulty

**Simultaneous Localization and Mapping.** To build a map you need to know where you are. To know
where you are you need a map. SLAM does both at once, and the reason it works is *constraints*: each
scan constrains the pose that could have produced it, given the map so far.

`slam_toolbox` in `mode: mapping` maintains a **pose graph** — nodes are past poses, edges are
constraints from scan matching — and optimises the whole graph whenever new information arrives. The
solver here is `CeresSolver` with `SPARSE_NORMAL_CHOLESKY`.

### 2. Loop closure is where the value is

Drive down a corridor and back. Odometry drifted, so the return journey does not land on the outward
one, and the map shows the same wall twice, slightly apart.

**Loop closure** recognises that a scan matches a place already in the graph, adds an edge between
two poses that were previously far apart in the graph, and re-optimises. The accumulated error is
distributed backwards over the whole loop.

That is why the acceptance gate is written the way it is:

> `slam_toolbox` closes a **≥ 30 m** hallway loop; repeated observations of the same wall agree
> within **≤ 10 cm**; the map loads in Nav2 without manual editing.

A short straight run proves nothing — drift has not had room to accumulate, and no loop has closed.

### 3. Reading `slam_toolbox.yaml`

```yaml
resolution: 0.05
max_laser_range: 12.0          # matches the RPLIDAR A1 profile in the URDF
minimum_travel_distance: 0.2
minimum_travel_heading: 0.2
scan_buffer_size: 15
map_update_interval: 1.0
```

!!! info "The thresholds are deliberately tighter than the defaults"

    From the file's own comment:

    > Thresholds are tighter than the defaults because this vehicle moves at ≤ walking speed:
    > waiting 0.5 m between scans would throw away most of the data on a slow indoor run.

    Defaults are tuned for robots that cover ground faster. Inheriting them here would silently
    discard most of your scans — and the map would just be *worse*, with nothing to indicate why.

    Also note `base_frame: base_link`, carrying the correction from the design verification:
    B-MROVER's SLAM used `base_footprint` while its EKF used `base_link`. **There is no
    `base_footprint` anywhere in this stack** — if you see one, something is importing an unported
    config.

### 4. Transform ownership, again

| Transform | Owner |
|---|---|
| `map → odom` | `slam_toolbox` — the accumulated drift correction |
| `odom → base_link` | EKF — the local pose estimate |
| `base_link → *` | `robot_state_publisher` |

`map → odom` is worth understanding properly: it is **not** the robot's pose. It is the correction
between where dead reckoning thinks the robot is and where SLAM believes it actually is. When loop
closure fires, that transform jumps — and that jump is correct behaviour, not a glitch.

### 5. Reading a bad map

| Symptom | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| **Doubled walls**, worse after turning | Bad sensor extrinsic — Lab 4's failure |
| **Smeared or blurred walls** | Timestamp/sync problem, or scans arriving faster than pose updates |
| Map drifts steadily, never corrects | Loop never closed — no revisited place |
| Map **jumps** and reshapes | Loop closure fired. Correct |
| Wide-open space where a wall is | Glass, or a surface below/above the scan plane |
| Robot "walks through" walls | TF broken, or two publishers of one transform |

!!! quote "The habit this table encodes"

    A bad map is **evidence about the pipeline**, not a verdict on SLAM. Each symptom above points at
    a different upstream component — extrinsics, clocks, sensing geometry, TF ownership.

    On this vehicle, add one more candidate to the top of your list: `mitt_dimensions.yaml` was
    estimated until this week. **A wrong wheelbase produces a wrong yaw rate, and therefore a
    heading-dependent map error that looks exactly like a bad extrinsic.**
